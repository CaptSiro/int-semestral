# Scenario

Outlines the specific situation the user may find themselves in

## Outline

Jaroslav (70) has painful finger joints. While waiting at his doctor's office he tries to open a document from his health insurer on his phone. 
Typing his password on the small on-screen keyboard hurts, he makes a typo, and he then has to get through two more identity checks. 
At home he could dictate, but he won't say his password out loud in a waiting room full of strangers. 
He gives up, puts the phone away and plans to try again in the evening.

**What he needs:** a way to enter passwords and other sensitive data that doesn't take repeated, painful finger movements and doesn't have to be said aloud. It should be comfortable, secure and private, and work wherever he is.

## Story

**Just Logging In**

It is Monday, shortly after three in the afternoon. 
Seventy-year-old Jaroslav is sitting in the waiting room at his general practitioner's office. 
He has about twenty minutes before his appointment, so he takes out his phone to check a message from his health insurance company. 
They have informed him that a new document is available in his online account. 
Jaroslav likes to take care of these things himself. 
He is used to keeping track of his own affairs, and he appreciates that he no longer has to make phone calls or visit offices in person for most of them.

He opens the link in the email and reaches the login page. 
He enters his username and moves on to his password. 
The keyboard takes up almost half the screen, and Jaroslav has to select each character slowly, one at a time. 
With every tap, he feels an uncomfortable strain in his fingers. 
His finger joints have been hurting more frequently lately, and typing for extended periods has become tiring. 
Still, he carries on. 
He does not want to be discouraged by something as ordinary as logging into an account.

After a while, he stops. 
He needs to switch to numbers and then back to letters. 
He tries to continue, but accidentally enters one of the characters incorrectly. 
He deletes it and starts correcting his password. 
When he finally manages to log in, he is asked to confirm his identity once again. 
Jaroslav leans back in his chair and closes his eyes for a moment. 
He has not even reached the document he wanted to access, yet he already feels as though using his phone has taken more effort than it should.

The waiting room is quiet, with only the occasional conversation at the reception desk. 
Jaroslav knows he could make tasks like this easier at home by using voice input. 
Here, however, he cannot imagine saying his login credentials aloud in front of strangers. 
It is not just that someone might overhear him. 
He considers his passwords private and does not want entering them to become something he has to explain or feel self-conscious about around other people.

When he finally accesses the portal, he discovers that he can only open the document after completing another identity verification step. 
A new field appears on the screen, asking him to enter his details. 
Jaroslav looks at his hands and rubs his fingers. 
He considers leaving it until the evening, but at the same time, he is frustrated that he has to postpone such a simple task. 
A phone is supposed to make everyday life easier, not turn a brief task into several minutes of effort and frustration.

He remembers a time when he used to handle most of these matters in person. 
He had to travel and wait in queues, but at least he did not have to struggle with tiny buttons and repeatedly enter individual characters. 
He has no desire to go back to that way of doing things. 
He likes being able to communicate with public institutions, manage his accounts, and access important information directly from his phone. 
He just wishes that the convenience offered by technology did not sometimes come with barriers of its own.

Jaroslav decides to open the document later. 
He locks his phone and puts it back in his pocket just as the nurse calls his name. 
He stands up and walks towards the examination room. 
He no longer thinks about the login page, but an unpleasant feeling lingers: something as routine as securely entering a few characters can affect what he is able to do and when he can do it.

He does not want to learn complicated new procedures or rely on his family every time he needs help with something minor. 
He wants to continue using his phone as naturally as anyone else and decide for himself when to take care of his affairs. 
He needs a way to enter passwords and other sensitive information without repeatedly making painful finger movements, while still protecting his privacy. 
For Jaroslav, a truly accessible phone would not simply be one that allows him to complete a task, but one that lets him do so comfortably, securely, and whenever he needs to.
